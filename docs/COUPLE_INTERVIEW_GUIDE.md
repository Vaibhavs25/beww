# Couple discovery and prototype testing guide

Companion to [RESEARCH_AND_PRODUCT_PLAN.md](./RESEARCH_AND_PRODUCT_PLAN.md). This is a proposed research protocol; no participants have been recruited or interviewed.

## Purpose

Find out what couples already do to feel connected, what they wish were easier, and whether the proposed product would fit their routines. Prioritize observed past behavior over enthusiastic predictions about a new app.

## Recruitment

Start with 12 couples: six nearby and six long-distance. Include a mix of relationship lengths, work/study schedules, Android/iPhone combinations, app enthusiasts, and people who abandoned previous couple apps. Avoid recruiting only friends who want to encourage the founder.

Invite each person to participate voluntarily. Let either person skip any question or stop. Do not collect private messages, intimate photos, passwords, precise locations, or call recordings. Use fictional examples during prototype tasks. If recording an interview is desired, obtain separate consent first.

## Part 1: individual interview — about 15 minutes per person

Ask these before showing features:

1. Tell me about the last time your partner made you smile when you were apart. What happened?
2. Walk me through a normal busy weekday. When do you usually connect with each other?
3. What do you use now for messages, photos, calls, games, plans, or memories? Which do you actually use each week?
4. When was the last time you wanted to do something together but could not decide what? What did you do instead?
5. Have you tried a couple app? What made you start? When did you last open it? What made you stop or keep using it?
6. When you are tired or stressed, how do you usually tell your partner what would help? Is there anything an app would make easier or more awkward?
7. Do you ever draw, send silly pictures, play games, or leave handwritten notes for each other? Tell me about a recent example.
8. What makes an app notification useful? What makes you turn notifications off?
9. Which information would you choose to share, and which should remain yours?
10. Have you paid for a shared app or activity recently? What made that worth paying for?

Follow up with “What happened next?”, “How often did that happen last month?”, and “What did you use instead?” Avoid asking participants to demonstrate private content.

## Part 2: joint prototype tasks — about 20 minutes

Use a prototype with fictional names and content. Do not explain each button before the task.

| Task | Observe |
| --- | --- |
| Choose nearby/long-distance and connect the pair | Whether the wording is clear; whether either person feels excluded; invite confusion |
| Share “overwhelmed, would like quiet time” | Whether the other person understands what was actually shared versus inferred |
| Send a photo or doodle and reply | Discoverability, number of steps, understanding of where it is saved |
| Begin a shared drawing; disconnect one device and return | Expectations about drafts, undo, the other person's work, and reconnection |
| Start a game during a call | Whether controls remain understandable and the call becomes distracting |
| Read a daily game result | Whether dates, wins, draws, and incomplete matches make sense |
| Read a quiz score or connection jar | What participants believe the percentage means, without prompting them |
| Change to nearby/long-distance later | Whether it is understood as a preference change rather than a new relationship |
| Find quiet hours, export, and unpair controls | Discoverability and expectations about existing shared content |

After each task, ask: “What did you expect?”, “What was confusing?”, and “Would this replace anything you already use?”

For the score, ask **“What does this number tell you?”** before asking whether they like it. If someone believes it measures love, loyalty, commitment, or relationship health, record that as a design failure to address.

## Part 3: prioritization — about 10 minutes

Give each person a list of the proposed features. Ask them separately to choose:

- Three they would keep if everything else disappeared.
- One they would likely use on a busy day.
- One they would use together on a free evening.
- One they dislike or would hide.
- One that might be enjoyable only in the first week.

Compare the two partners' choices without presenting disagreement as a relationship problem. Ask what could make the app useful if one partner uses it less often.

Only after discussing real needs, show concrete free/paid package concepts. Do not interpret “I might pay” as a purchase commitment. Record what the participant would give up or replace to pay for it.

## Two-week beta diary

Use 20 consenting couples, ten in each mode, for a small exploratory beta. This sample is for learning rather than population estimates.

Day 1: observe pairing and the first shared activity.

Day 3: ask what has been confusing or delightful, and fix severe defects.

Day 7: ask what they returned to voluntarily, what they ignored, and whether either partner felt pressured.

Day 14: ask what they would miss if the app disappeared, what they prefer doing in another app, and whether they want to continue.

Allow neutral or negative feedback in private from each partner. Do not send repeated reminders to make the research engagement numbers look better.

## Findings template

For each couple record an anonymous identifier, mode, device combination, broad relationship-duration range, and scheduling context. Avoid unnecessary personal details.

For each finding record:

| Field | What belongs here |
| --- | --- |
| Observation | A specific thing the participant did or described |
| Context | Nearby/long-distance, busy/free time, before/after trying the feature |
| Evidence type | Observed behavior, reported past behavior, or hypothetical preference |
| Feature implication | What we should preserve, change, or test |
| Counterexample | Who wanted the opposite or did not encounter the problem |
| Confidence | How consistently it appeared, without overstating the sample |

Keep prototype score variants clearly experimental. Test a quiz match percentage and a nonnumeric heart jar with different participants or alternate their order; a small qualitative test does not establish statistical superiority.

## Decisions after the beta

Keep a feature when couples return to it voluntarily and can explain its value. Simplify a feature when the idea is liked but the flow is confusing. Defer a feature when initial curiosity does not turn into repeat use. Rework or remove a feature that users repeatedly mistake for a judgment about their relationship.

Report nearby and long-distance patterns separately, including exceptions. Use the findings to revise the launch scope before increasing the game catalog or adding more engagement mechanics.
